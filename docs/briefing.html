Drones on Canadian farms, fact-checked

A research report recommended a three-tier drone fleet, a Raspberry Pi edge stack and six budget scenarios. Its prices were accurate to the dollar. Four of its regulatory claims were not.

What changed

Two rules landed. A third is pending.

The window that made agricultural drones practical in Canada opened inside twelve months, which is also why anything written about it needs a review date.

4 Nov 2025

Level 1 Complex BVLOS created

Final phase of SOR/2025-70 in force. Defined lower-risk beyond-visual-line-of-sight operations, and extended Advanced privileges to medium drones, EVLOS and sheltered operations. BVLOS requires an RPAS Operator Certificate — an accountable executive, SOPs, a maintenance programme.

8 Jun 2026

NPA 2026-005 published

Proposes mandatory Remote ID scaled by weight — FPV and model aircraft included — a community-organisation framework, and a new power to designate restricted drone airspace below 122 m / 400 ft.

30 Jun 2026

Health Canada SPN2026-02

A drone may apply any pesticide already registered for conventional aerial application, with no separate RPAS label amendment. Products that are not registered for aerial application, or that prohibit RPAS, remain off-limits.

9 Sep 2026

NPA 2026-005 comments closed

Four days before the report was dated. There is no longer a consultation to monitor; the next event is a final rule.

Corrections

The four errors

Ordered by consequence. The first one can put an operator outside the label.

Compliance risk

Spray buffer zones

Report said Follow “the aerial label’s application rate, spray volume, retreatment interval, buffer zones and other conditions.”
SPN2026-02 says Apply the buffer zones established for conventionally piloted rotary aircraft — an interim precaution until RPAS-specific drift models exist.

Why it mattersMany aerial labels carry separate fixed-wing and rotary buffers. The rotary figure is the binding one and is usually the larger. An operator following the report’s generic wording could apply the fixed-wing buffer and be both non-compliant and environmentally wrong.

Wrong date

NPA 2026-005 timing

Report said Published August 2026; treat it as an open proposal.
Actually Published 8 June 2026. Comments closed 9 September 2026.

Why it mattersThe action changes. Not “monitor the consultation” but “assume Remote ID is coming, budget the retrofit, and do not design an airframe that cannot accept a broadcast module.”

Scoped too narrowly

The omitted airspace power

Report said NPA 2026-005 is a Remote ID proposal.
Actually It is three things. The third is a power to designate restricted RPAS airspace below 122 m — the exact band every agricultural mission occupies.

Why it mattersA designated low-altitude restriction over or beside your fields would constrain operations regardless of certificate level. For anyone committing six figures, this is the most consequential pending change, and it is absent from the report entirely.

Understated cost

Level 1 Complex certification

Report said C$50.85 exam + C$125 issuance.
Actually Those fees are right, and they are the small part. Add the prerequisite Advanced exam, 20 h of purchased ground school, and a flight review charged by the reviewer. Four figures per pilot.

Why it mattersThe fee paragraph as written invites a reader to plan on C$176 for something that costs an order of magnitude more.

Peak payload printed beside peak endurance

The platform table gives the Matrice 400 “up to 6 kg” and “up to 59 min” in adjacent cells. Both figures are correct. They are also mutually exclusive.

The recommendation, gated

The missing instrument

Where the VTOL crossover actually sits

The report insists on an ROI gate and supplies no arithmetic to test it — no cost per hectare, no hectares per flight, no worked example. The tier structure rests on area efficiency, so here is the number it turns on.

Two break-even calculations the report should have carried

Stage 1, on 2,000 ha. C$12,000 of year-one capital over three years is C$4,000/yr. Flown five times a season, roughly C$0.40 per hectare-pass before labour. A 2% nitrogen-rate improvement on a C$120/ha nitrogen bill returns C$2.40/ha — about C$4,800/yr. One avoided blanket fungicide pass on 500 ha at C$50/ha returns C$25,000. It pays back inside a season on plausible numbers; if it does not on yours, do not proceed to stage 3.

Stage 6, custom spraying. At roughly C$45/ha billed less C$15/ha operating, margin is C$30/ha. Against C$45,000 year-one cost, break-even is about 1,500 ha in year one. A drone sprayer wins on wet or soft ground, small and irregular parcels, steep sites, and late-canopy timing where a ground rig causes damage. It loses badly on open, dry, large broad-acre. If your acres are flat, dry and large, do not buy one.

Illustrative. Replace every assumption with your own crop, area and input costs — that substitution is the point of the exercise.

Also missing

Six capital scenarios, zero operating cost

A first-year budget built from the report’s tables will under-forecast, and it under-forecasts most on the spray scenario — the most expensive one.

  • Software renewal. The M3M bundle includes three months of DJI Terra and one year of SmartFarm. Year two is a fresh line item.
  • Insurance. Not mentioned once in 375 lines. Commercial RPAS liability is effectively required at RPOC scale and is frequently demanded by landowners and clients.
  • Batteries as consumables. Agras packs have a finite cycle life and are a leading annual cost, yet they are counted once, as capital.
  • RPOC upkeep. Maintenance programme, document control, recurrent training every two years, accountable-executive time — for a thing the report itself says to treat as an aviation programme.
  • Connectivity. LTE only where coverage justifies it; otherwise store-and-forward, which is a design decision, not an afterthought.
Architecture

What runs where

The report gets this split right, and the split is the most common way these systems fail. Edge first for flight-time decisions, workstation first for photogrammetry, cloud for scale.

Kept from the report

Three rules worth enforcing in code

These are the report’s best judgements, and they are the kind that get quietly dropped during implementation. Each is testable, so each should be tested.

01 · read-only MAVLink

The Pi cannot command the aircraft

The autopilot owns flight; the companion computer owns perception. The aircraft must fly its failsafes correctly with the Pi rebooted and the network gone — so the telemetry link exposes no method that could arm, change mode or upload a mission, and a test asserts none appears.

02 · validated_locally

A model is a sensor, not a decision

DeepWeeds learned Australian species in Australian conditions; PlantVillage is single leaves on plain backgrounds, not aerial imagery. Neither is evidence about Saskatchewan canola. A false positive costs a walk across a field — or, wired to a sprayer, chemical, crop and an environmental incident.

03 · DEGRADED, not deleted

Failed QA flags data, it does not discard it

A blurred frame over a real anomaly is still evidence that something is there. Deciding to throw data away belongs to an agronomist on the ground with context, not to a threshold on a Pi at 100 m AGL.

Before anything is ordered

Still unspecified

Crop, average field size, province, connectivity, target ground-sampling distance, and whether the commercial value is stress detection, stand counting, weed mapping, water management, variable-rate prescription, documentation or spraying.

Each of these can move the recommended system by a full tier. The report is right that mission architecture comes first and airframe second — which means the honest next step is not a purchase order but a decision about what question the imagery is being bought to answer.
